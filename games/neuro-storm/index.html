What you are looking at

In the GPU modes (55K to 1M neurons) this is a layered cortex. Both hemispheres are covered by a folded grey-matter sheet 2.5 mm thick, with the central sulcus, the Sylvian fissure and the longitudinal fissure, joined by the corpus callosum. The Lobes option tints the frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital lobes.

  • Six layers. Layer I is almost cell-free. II/III holds pyramidal cells. IV is granular and densest, with spiny stellate cells. V holds large pyramidal cells. VI holds corticothalamic pyramidal cells. Shares are I 1%, II/III 39%, IV 17%, V 17% and VI 26%.
  • Seven cell types, with 80% excitatory and 20% inhibitory cells. Excitatory cells are regular-spiking with adaptation. PV basket cells are fast-spiking, with a short refractory period and strong, fast perisomatic IPSPs. SST/Martinotti cells are adapting and low-threshold, and send slow IPSPs to the upper layers. VIP cells are disinhibitory and mainly inhibit SST cells.
  • Canonical microcircuit. Thalamus → IV → II/III → V → VI → IV, plus long horizontal II/III connections and homotopic callosal fibres. Each neuron integrates about 700–2,200 synapses (the average is shown above). Each one gives a 0.2–1 mV PSP, with conduction delays that grow with distance.
  • Thalamic → L4 mode delivers a sensory volley into layer IV. Activity then spreads up to II/III, down to V/VI, and then sideways.
  • The Layers view in the inspector is a slice through the sheet under the selected cell, with every simulated neuron of the patch at its true depth. Cutaway clips the brain at the selected cell.

How it runs: the whole network is simulated on the GPU (WebGL2 float textures, 0.05 ms steps). Synapses are not stored one by one. Each neuron draws its synaptic input every step from the spikes of each presynaptic population around it, using distance-dependent Gaussian connection fields, per-population in-degrees and axonal delays (a statistically exact mean-field "binomial" draw of its ~2,000 inputs). Every cell has hashed individual thresholds and positions. Simplifications: layers II and III are merged, layer I neurons are modelled as VIP-like, the folding is procedural, and the sheet area (~840 cm²) is smaller than a real cortex (~1,800 cm²), so each model neuron still stands in for a few real ones.

The single neuron (all modes)

Each glowing dot is a neuron in the cortical grey matter of both hemispheres. Cyan cells are excitatory pyramidal cells (80%) and magenta cells are inhibitory fast-spiking interneurons (20%, Dale's law: each cell has only one kind of output). Brightness follows the membrane potential. A cell brightens as EPSPs depolarise it, flashes white when it fires, glows ember-orange while it is refractory, and turns dim blue when IPSPs or the after-hyperpolarisation push it below rest.

The neuron

  • Resting potential −70 mV: K⁺ leak conductance with EL = −70 mV, membrane time constant 10 ms (pyramidal) or 6 ms (interneuron), C = 1 µF/cm².
  • Threshold ≈ −55 mV (±1.2 mV cell-to-cell). When V crosses it the cell fires an action potential: a 0.35 ms upstroke to +35 mV (interneurons +28 mV, narrower spike), then repolarisation.
  • After-hyperpolarisation: a K⁺ conductance (EK = −90 mV) opens with each spike, pulls V down to about −79 mV and then decays. A slow K⁺ adaptation current (pyramidal cells only, τ 180 ms) lets V creep back to −70 over ~170 ms.
  • Absolute refractory period (2.0 ms pyramidal, 1.2 ms interneuron): Na⁺ channels are inactivated. No input, however strong, can fire the cell.
  • Relative refractory period (about 2 to 17 ms and fading): the threshold jumps by 22 mV and decays with τ 5 ms, while the AHP holds V below rest. Only a stronger input fires the cell. Threshold and V then return to baseline.

The synapses

  • EPSP: AMPA-like conductance, reversal 0 mV, decay τ 3 ms (about +4 mV at rest).
  • IPSP: GABAA-like Cl⁻ conductance, reversal −80 mV, decay τ 7 ms. Near rest it is only a small hyperpolarisation, but near threshold it is strongly shunting.
  • Each cell gets about 56 excitatory and 9 inhibitory inputs (the inhibitory synapses are fewer but stronger, as for real perisomatic basket-cell synapses), mostly from close neighbours (Gaussian fall-off with distance), plus a few fast callosal fibres from the other hemisphere. Axonal delays grow with distance (0.6 ms synaptic + conduction). You can watch the light pulses run along the axons.

Why balance matters

A pulse fires a small patch of cells. Their EPSPs summate in neighbours, which fire and pass the wave on. The interneurons are recruited too, and their IPSPs, together with every cell's refractoriness and AHP, make the wave shrink and die out. The cortex then returns to its quiet resting state. Turn inhibition down and the same pulse becomes a runaway, seizure-like storm (at 0% it never stops). Turn it up and the spread is blocked. Raising noise enough makes the network ignite spontaneously.

Reading the inspector

The trace shows V (mV) of the selected cell. Dashed lines mark rest (−70) and threshold (−55), and the orange line is the moving threshold. Red shading marks the absolute refractory period and amber shading the relative one. ▲ marks an arriving EPSP and ▼ an arriving IPSP, and ⚡ marks your stimulus. The bottom strip is a raster: one row per neuron, sorted front to back, left hemisphere above and right below. The line on it is the population firing rate.

Model: conductance-based leaky integrate-and-fire with an explicit spike waveform, AHP and adaptation K⁺ currents and a dynamic threshold. Fixed 0.05 ms time step, several substeps per frame, deterministic seeds. Each model cell stands in for a small group of real neurons, so unitary PSPs are larger than a typical single cortical synapse, and time runs in slow motion (see Simulation speed).
